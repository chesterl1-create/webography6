Forensic DNA Databases and Biometric Identification (CODIS)

URL https://www.fbi.gov/how-we-can-help-you/dna-fingerprint-act-of-2005-expungement-policy/codis-and-ndis-fact-sheet
Site Author Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI)
Abstract This official fact sheet from the Federal Bureau of Investigation explains how the Combined DNA Index System and National DNA Index System support forensic investigations across federal, state, and local crime laboratories. It describes how analysts upload short tandem repeat profiles from crime scene evidence, convicted offenders, and arrestees to generate investigative leads and connect related cases. The resource clarifies how agencies verify database hits using court-authorized reference samples and explains why hit counts reflect investigative leads rather than final court convictions. Students and researchers studying criminal justice technology can use this page to understand standardized DNA indexing protocols, laboratory quality rules, and multi-jurisdictional information sharing. It provides authoritative federal context on how genetic databases assist investigators while maintaining structured procedural safeguards and oversight across participating forensic laboratories.

URL https://www.fjc.gov/content/361262/law-enforcement-databases-limited-genetic-information-and-varying-procedures-use
Site Author Federal Judicial Center (FJC)
Abstract Published by the Federal Judicial Center, this judicial resource examines how law enforcement agencies collect, store, and search genetic information across different database types. It contrasts limited short tandem repeat profiles used in law enforcement indices with broader genomic data and direct-to-consumer genealogy resources that raise distinct privacy considerations. The page outlines practices such as familial searching and partial match reviews alongside varying statutory rules governing arrestee collection and expungement. It also highlights legal and scientific questions judges and practitioners encounter when evaluating match statistics, database reliability, and civil liberties protections. For readers exploring the history and modern evolution of forensic identification, this site offers clear context on balancing investigative efficiency with constitutional privacy rights and procedural accountability.

Automated License Plate Readers and Body-Worn Camera Surveillance

URL https://www.dhs.gov/science-and-technology/saver/automatic-license-plate-readers
Site Author U.S. Department of Homeland Security (DHS) Science and Technology Directorate
Abstract This technical overview from the Department of Homeland Security System Assessment and Validation for Emergency Responders program details how Automated License Plate Recognition systems function in public safety operations. It explains how high-speed cameras and optical character recognition software capture license plate images, convert characters into searchable text, and check records against watchlists for stolen vehicles or warrants. The resource notes how modern computer vision and artificial intelligence improvements have increased recognition accuracy, lowered costs, and expanded deployment options. By organizing equipment types and technical criteria, the page helps readers understand how automated vehicle tracking fits into contemporary policing infrastructure. It serves as a reliable government reference for examining operational capabilities and technical standards surrounding automated plate-reading tools.

URL https://www.policingproject.org/automated-license-plate-readers
Site Author The Policing Project at NYU School of Law
Abstract This policy resource from The Policing Project examines how Automated License Plate Readers and related surveillance technologies are used in policing and how they affect civil liberties. It discusses investigative benefits alongside concerns such as continuous location tracking, disparate impacts in heavily policed communities, and alerts based on outdated or inaccurate database entries. The page shares policy guides, legislative scorecards, and model rules emphasizing data retention limits, public transparency, and independent oversight. It also highlights legal disputes challenging unchecked surveillance expansion by law enforcement agencies. For students studying twenty-first-century policing technology, this site shows how legal scholars and accountability advocates design governance guardrails to protect privacy while supporting legitimate public safety goals.

Predictive Policing and Pretrial Algorithmic Risk Assessment

URL https://www.brennancenter.org/our-work/research-reports/predictive-policing-explained
Site Author Tim Lau, Brennan Center for Justice at NYU School of Law
Abstract This report from the Brennan Center for Justice explains how predictive policing tools use algorithms and historical records to forecast crime locations or individuals associated with elevated risk. It distinguishes between place-based forecasting that maps prior incident patterns and person-based models that score arrest or victimization risk factors. While noting arguments about resource efficiency and moving beyond subjective hunches, the analysis explains how training data shaped by past policing practices can perpetuate racial disparities. It also points out that lack of transparency and independent evaluations have led several departments to end or limit predictive policing initiatives. This resource offers clear analysis of the civil rights risks and empirical limitations of algorithmic forecasting in policing.

URL https://www.amu.apus.edu/area-of-study/criminal-justice/resources/what-is-predictive-policing/
Site Author American Military University (Criminal Justice Faculty)
Abstract This educational article from American Military University introduces how data analytics, social network analysis, and cloud computing support predictive policing efforts across law enforcement agencies. It outlines potential operational uses such as patrol scheduling, violence reduction, investigative prioritization, and threat monitoring aimed at helping agencies allocate limited resources proactively. At the same time, the article raises concerns about surveillance creep, public accountability, and constitutional probable cause standards. It stresses that algorithmic outputs should serve as a decision-support aid rather than replacing human judgment, legal standards, and community oversight. Readers can use this overview to examine practical applications alongside legal and ethical debates surrounding predictive algorithms in criminal justice.
